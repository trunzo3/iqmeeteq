The pattern

The rollout happened. Nothing moved.

MoreLess

Most public organizations are not short on effort or expertise. What they lack is a way to turn a decision into what people do every day.

So the mandate gets announced, the sessions get delivered, and attendance gets tracked. Six months later people have quietly gone back to the old way. Nobody failed. There was just no plan to keep the change going.

The structure

Nobody buys training. They buy change.

MoreLess

A workshop with no plan around it is over when people leave the room. Anything worth training people on deserves a full plan: decisions, messaging, leadership, adoption, and follow-through.

So we build every engagement as a change effort first. Before we deliver any content, we design what gets decided, how it gets communicated, who reinforces it, how it's measured, and what happens when people resist.

That's why the same approach works for a statewide AI rollout and for two nonprofits that can't agree on a shared client intake process. The subject changes. The structure doesn't.

Why this is harder here

Change fails differently depending on who you answer to.

  • Leadership and capacity work reaching more than 40 HHS managers across California
  • AI governance for executives and hands-on AI training for their teams, inside California state agencies
  • Facilitation between two partnering nonprofits to rebuild a working agreement
MoreLess

When you are accountable to statute, a change has to pass audits and legislative review, so it has to be documented as carefully as it is designed. When you are accountable to members, your tolerance for risk is low, so governance has to come first. When you are accountable to a board and funders, you have to defend the change in a grant report and a board meeting before anyone sees a result.

Most of you are carrying more than one of these at the same time. That is the actual complexity, and it is what a private-sector change playbook does not account for.

Where the methods come from

Our methods come from fifteen years of building systems that had to produce results under quarterly pressure.

Private-sector precision. Public-service mission. Systems that last.

These frameworks were developed inside high-growth and Fortune 100 organizations, environments where a leadership program that didn't change behavior was cancelled, not repeated. They're based on decision science and were tested in places that measured results closely.

Public service has harder constraints and higher stakes, and it deserves the same rigor. That's the whole premise: private-sector precision, public-service mission.
